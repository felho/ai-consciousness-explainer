# **Consciousness as supervisor of in-context learning**

Human consciousness is highly circumscribed. As the computer scientist [Joscha Bach](https://cimc.ai/cimcHypothesis.pdf) puts it, our phenomenology occupies a “bubble of nowness” that integrates our thoughts and valence-laden perceptions into a coherent, unified thread of experience. Consciousness also has a second-order quality, i.e. we can notice ourselves noticing things. These and related points lead to Bach’s conjecture that consciousness isn’t merely related to a coherent self-model, but is itself the “[coherence inducing operator](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qoHCQ1ozswA)” that underlies our capacity for learning in the first place.

We need to be conscious to learn at all, and are more conscious when learning something new than when doing something we’ve already mastered. A novice driver is aware of every gear shift, mirror check, and brake input, while an experienced driver can drive across town as if on autopilot. Our conscious attention tends to fall on novelty, prediction errors, and whatever our brain has not yet distilled into learned habits. Remove it and we become useless: an incoherent sleep-walker at best; anesthetized or dead at worst.

According to [Attention Schema Theory](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4407481/), consciousness just *is* the brain’s imperfect model of its own attention. While the [prefrontal cortex](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-61399-5) handles top-down attentional control, the attention schema that accounts for our subjective awareness is delocalized across regions of the [temporoparietal junction and superior temporal sulcus](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7713666/), particularly those responsible for social cognition.[![image](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Ng8c!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa3347e10-2cd2-4c46-a5ff-e0bb641956f4_1024x1536.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Ng8c!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa3347e10-2cd2-4c46-a5ff-e0bb641956f4_1024x1536.png)Derived from Graziano’s “[Consciousness Engineered](https://grazianolab.princeton.edu/sites/g/files/toruqf3411/files/graziano/files/jcs_graziano_2016.pdf)” with the author’s permission.

Consciousness seems particularly useful to our brain’s ongoing self-supervised learning process. Rather than learning in a blind, trial-and-error-like fashion, consciously attending to prediction errors augments reward signals with an interpretation of what we got wrong. The prefrontal cortex then sees its [activation drop](https://www.jneurosci.org/content/25/22/5356) after a skill has been learned and automatized.

Analogous techniques are used to train reasoning models and AI agents. Instead of giving models sparse rewards for correct outputs (output supervision), fine-grained reward signals can be applied at each reasoning step or token (process supervision) to greatly improve performance. More generally, LLMs are commonly used as a judge to rank and filter their own outputs and improve the signal from supervised fine-tuning. If our attention schema effectively implements an internalized form of process supervision and data labeling under a higher-order RL policy, developing an attention schema (and hence a capacity for subjective experience) may be essential for continual learning in AIs, too, if it’s not already present during in-context learning.

The line between in-context and continual learning is fuzzy in both AIs and humans. Synaptic weight changes in biological brains require de novo [protein synthesis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18309046/), which occurs on the timescale of [hours](https://elifesciences.org/articles/46356) to days. Our conscious, in-the-moment learning instead leverages the persistent activity of our working memory and other short-term [synaptic dynamics](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1150769), letting the brain behave as if it had updated its weights. The real weight updates seem to instead happen [during sleep](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8036517/), when [hippocampal replay](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn1825) drives [cortical reactivation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24904169/) in compressed time, performing the [analog](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3921176/) of batch gradient descent over the day’s [tagged experiences](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9020359/). 

Similarly, the powerful in-context learning capacity of the transformer model lies in the self-attention mechanism, which enables [gradient learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.07677) within-context through the equivalent of *[virtual](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11174)*[weight updates](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11174). This suggests that consciousness is compatible with an otherwise frozen neural network, while full continual learning requires a [complementary learning system](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22141588/) for distilling experiences over longer time-scales or in batches.
